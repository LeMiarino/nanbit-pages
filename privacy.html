Nanbit Privacy Policy

Effective 13 September 2026. Nanbit is made by Denver Delorino, an individual developer.

Nanbit is a habit tracker. It is built to keep the things you track on your phone. This policy explains exactly what is stored, the few things that leave the device, and what would change if optional accounts arrive later.

The short version

  • Your habits, your completions and your settings are stored on your device. Nanbit never uploads them, and there is no Nanbit server that holds them. If your phone backs itself up, that backup can include them — see section 5 below.
  • Nanbit has no analytics about how you use the app, no advertising, no tracking, and no crash-reporting service.
  • Nanbit uses a service called RevenueCat to manage subscriptions. In a version of Nanbit configured to sell them, its software runs whenever the app is open, so a small request goes to RevenueCat when Nanbit starts, whether or not you ever subscribe. It carries a random identifier for the installation and nothing about your habits. A version that is not configured to sell subscriptions does not contact RevenueCat at all.
  • If you buy a Nanbit subscription, the store you buy it through processes the payment: Apple on iPhone, Google Play on Android.
  • If the app crashes, the next launch may ask Expo's update service for a corrected version of its own code, and that request includes the text of the error. It does not check at any other time.
  • Nanbit does not sell or share your data.

What Nanbit stores on your device

All of this lives in a database inside the app's own storage, which only Nanbit can read:

  • Habits — the name, icon, colour, schedule, daily goal and reminder time you set.
  • Completions — which habit you marked done, on which day, and how many times.
  • Settings — your display name, appearance choice, which day your week starts on, the streak grace-day switch, your reminder preferences, and some bookkeeping such as whether you have finished the introduction. Two fields exist for the optional accounts described at the end of this policy and are empty in this version. The display name is whatever you type; Nanbit never asks for your real name.
  • A summary cache — daily totals worked out from the above, kept so the charts are fast. It contains nothing new.
  • Records you have deleted. When you delete a habit or clear a day, Nanbit marks the record as deleted and hides it everywhere in the app, but the row stays in the database file. This is groundwork for the optional sync described below: a device has to be able to learn that something was deleted. Nanbit never sends it anywhere, though a backup your phone makes copies the database whole, deleted records included. Reset all data removes it along with everything else.
  • Export and import files, briefly. Exporting writes the file into the app's own temporary storage before handing it to the share sheet, and importing copies the file you choose into the same place. Both are deleted as soon as the operation is over — whether you went through with it or not — so neither is left sitting on the device.

Nanbit sends none of this anywhere. Deleting the app removes it from the phone, but not from a backup the phone has already made — see section 5.

What leaves your device

Four things that Nanbit sends, and nothing else. A fifth is sent by your phone rather than by Nanbit: its own backup, described last.

1. Subscriptions, and the app knowing whether you have one

Nanbit uses RevenueCat to tell whether a subscription is active. Whether it contacts RevenueCat at all is settled when a version of the app is built: a version configured to sell subscriptions contacts it, and a version that is not does not contact it at all — not at launch, not when you open the subscription screen, not at any other time.

  • Every time you open Nanbit, and every time you return to it, the app asks RevenueCat what this installation is entitled to. This happens whether or not you have ever subscribed, and whether or not you have ever opened the subscription screen. What is sent is a random identifier RevenueCat generates for this installation of the app, along with ordinary technical details such as the app and operating-system version, and — as with any internet request — your device's IP address. Nothing about your habits is ever included. It cannot be: the app never gives RevenueCat any of your data.
  • If you subscribe, the store handles the purchase and the payment: Apple on iPhone, Google Play on Android. Nanbit never sees your card details, your Apple Account or Google account, your name or your email address. Each store's handling of your data is covered by its own policy: Apple's Privacy Policy and Google's Privacy Policy.
  • RevenueCat, acting on our behalf, checks the purchase with the store it was made in — Apple or Google Play — and tells the app whether the subscription is active. What is processed is your purchase history — which product was bought, when, and whether it is still active or in a trial — tied to that same random installation identifier. It is not your Apple Account or Google account, and it is not linked to your name or email. RevenueCat's handling of it is covered by RevenueCat's Privacy Policy.

This is used for two things: unlocking paid features and restoring your purchase on a new device, and aggregate subscription figures such as how many people subscribed in a month. It is not used for advertising, and it is not used to track you across other apps or websites.

2. Recovering from a crash

If Nanbit stops unexpectedly, the next launch can repair the app by fetching a corrected version of its own code from Expo's update service. It does not check at any other time — not on a normal launch, and not while the app is running.

That request carries the platform, the app version, a random identifier for this installation, and the text of the error that caused the crash: the error message and the programmer-facing trace of where in Nanbit's own code it happened, cut off after about a thousand characters. This is what makes it possible to tell whether a fix exists for the problem you actually hit. It is not a report about you or your habits, and there is no crash-reporting service or dashboard behind it — but it is error text leaving the device, and we would rather say so than describe the request as carrying less than it does. Expo's handling of it is covered by Expo's Privacy Policy.

The random identifier here is generated by the app. It is not your Apple Account or Google account, not an advertising identifier, and not connected to any account.

3. Backups you export yourself

You → Export data writes a copy of your records and hands it to your phone's share sheet. Where it goes from there is entirely your choice — a file, a message, another app. Nanbit does not upload it, and does not know what you did with it.

What the copy holds depends on which you pick. JSON carries your habits, archived ones included, your completions, and your settings — which include the display name you typed in. CSV carries one row per completion: the date, the habit's name, the count, the goal, and whether you met it. Neither carries the records you have deleted, and neither carries the summary cache, which holds nothing that is not worked out from the rest.

4. Links you tap

The support, privacy and terms links open your browser at an ordinary web address. Nothing about you or your habits is attached; your browser behaves as it always does.

5. Your phone's own backup, which Nanbit does not send

If your phone backs itself up — iCloud Backup on iPhone, Google's backup on Android — that backup can contain Nanbit's database: your habits, completions and settings, and the deleted records described above. Nanbit does not send it and cannot read it. It goes to your own Apple or Google account under your phone's backup settings, and Apple's or Google's privacy policy covers it rather than this one.

What is known differs by platform. On iPhone, Apple documents that iCloud Backup includes the data of apps you have downloaded, and Nanbit keeps its database where that applies. On Android it is expected rather than confirmed: Nanbit uses Android's standard backup settings, which include the app's own storage, but no restore has been tested yet.

So deleting Nanbit, or using Reset all data, clears the phone but not a backup the phone has already made. What happens to that backup is up to your phone's backup settings, not to Nanbit.

What Nanbit does not do

  • No analytics about how you use the app. Nothing measures which screens you open, which habits you keep, or how often you come back. The only aggregate figures that exist anywhere are RevenueCat's subscription counts, described above.
  • No advertising, and no advertising identifiers.
  • No tracking, as Apple defines it: nothing about you is linked to data from other companies' apps or websites, for any purpose.
  • No crash-reporting service. The one exception is the update request described in section 2, which carries the last error's text.
  • No accounts, and no server holding your habits.
  • No selling, renting or sharing of personal data.
  • No location, contacts, photos, microphone, camera or health data. Nanbit never asks for them because it never uses them.

Notifications

Reminders are scheduled by your phone, locally, from the habits already on your device. There is no push server and no push token: nothing about a reminder leaves the phone, and Nanbit cannot send you anything you did not schedule yourself. Because a reminder names the habit it is for, a habit's name can appear on your lock screen — that is your phone displaying it, not Nanbit sending it anywhere. You can turn notifications off at any time in the app or in your phone's settings.

On Android, reminders rely on two permissions. One lets Nanbit post notifications (POST_NOTIFICATIONS); Android 13 and later ask you for it. The other lets Nanbit start when the phone does (RECEIVE_BOOT_COMPLETED), so that reminders you have already scheduled are set again after a restart. Neither gives Nanbit access to anything else on your phone.

Your choices and controls

  • See what Nanbit holds — it is all visible in the app, and You → Export data gives you a machine-readable copy of your habits, completions and settings, described in section 3. It does not include the records you have deleted, which the app has already stopped showing you everywhere. Export stays free whether or not you subscribe, so you are never locked away from your own records.
  • Hide individual records — deleting a habit removes it and its completions from everywhere in the app. As described above, the underlying rows remain in the database file on your device until you reset or remove the app.
  • Delete everything, properly — You → About Nanbit → Reset all data erases the database, deleted records included, and returns the app to a fresh install. Removing the app does the same. Neither reaches a backup your phone has already made (section 5).
  • Manage or cancel a subscription — on iPhone, open the Settings app, tap your name, then tap Subscriptions; on Android, go to subscriptions in Google Play. Cancelling never deletes anything you have tracked.
  • Ask us to delete purchase records — the subscription records held by RevenueCat are keyed to a random installation identifier. If you want them erased, write to delorinodenver@gmail.com and we will action it. You may need to tell us roughly when and what you purchased, since we have no other way to find you.

How to reach us about your data

Write to delorinodenver@gmail.com. That address reaches Denver Delorino, who makes Nanbit, and it is the only route — there is no support desk and no ticketing system behind it.

Because Nanbit holds no account and no contact details for you, we usually cannot look you up. For anything except a purchase record there is nothing for us to find: your habits are on your phone and we have never had a copy. For a purchase record, tell us roughly what you bought and when. A reply normally takes a few days.

If you are in the UK or the EU, you have rights of access, correction, erasure, restriction, objection and portability over personal data. In practice those rights bite only on the purchase records described above, since we hold nothing else about you — and your habits are already portable through Export data, without asking anyone.

How long data is kept

Data on your device is kept until you delete it or remove the app; a copy in your phone's own backup is kept under your backup settings (section 5). Records you delete inside the app are hidden immediately and stay in the database file until you use Reset all data or remove the app. Purchase records are kept by the store you bought through — Apple or Google — and by RevenueCat, for as long as needed to support restores, refunds and financial record-keeping.

Children

Nanbit is not directed at children under 13. It collects no name, no email, no age and no contact details from anyone. You can type a display name into the app if you want one — it is whatever you choose, Nanbit never asks for a real name, and it is not sent to us or to anybody else. The only information Nanbit sends off the device is described above: a random per-installation identifier, subscription status, and — after a crash — the text of the error. Nothing in that identifies a child. The display name travels only in an export you make yourself (section 3) and in a backup your phone makes (section 5), both of which are yours.

What changes if sync arrives

Nanbit may later add optional accounts so habits can sync between your devices. If that happens:

  • It will stay optional. Nothing in Nanbit will be gated behind an account, and buying or restoring a subscription will never require one.
  • If, and only if, you choose to sign in, your habits and completions would be stored on a server so they can reach your other devices, and your purchase history would become linked to that account rather than to a random identifier.
  • This policy will be updated, and the app will say what is happening, before any of that ships. Nothing about the version you have today changes retroactively.

Changes to this policy

If this policy changes, the date at the top changes with it. That date is the whole record — there is no notice, because there is no account to send one to, and promising one we could not deliver would be worse than saying so.
